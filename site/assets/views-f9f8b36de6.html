The sky, reviewed.

SetuHQ searches any TESS star's public light curve for transits. A calibrated panel of Sentinels (AI reviewers) and Probes (deterministic detectors) marks the dips, and vetting explains every verdict. Nothing is called a planet until it is one.

What this page does not claim

  • No confirmed planet. Every dip, signal and feed entry here is a candidate, unconfirmed. Passing vetting is not a confirmation. The match with pi Men c says the planet is already known, not that this run confirmed it.
  • No planet sizes. No stellar radius is stored, so a depth gives only a planet-to-star radius ratio. The radius-ratio page shows the working.
  • No people on the panel. The panel is the Sentinels and the Probes: 2 Sentinels and 3 Probes. Sentinels run on Claude (Anthropic) by default; other providers are supported and labelled. Probes are deterministic matched-filter detectors. Their weights rest on calibration with 10 planted and 10 empty light curves each, so they are rough.
  • Archive values only. This page claims no detection. A TOI or archive match compares a stored period with published values.
  • No image of the planet. Planet pictures are artist's impressions, chosen from measured radius and irradiation; How the pictures are drawn ›

Sentinels run on Claude (Anthropic) by default; other providers are supported and labelled. Probes are deterministic matched-filter detectors. Weights come from 10 planted and 10 empty light curves each: too few to separate 0.6 from 0.5.

      
      
Measured on real TOIs
The live feed's new signals are consistent with noise. Noise alone should give about 12.3 false new_candidate signals per 100 targets reviewed, against 15.8 per 100 on the live feed. Under that earlier rule a new_candidate on the feed was more likely noise than a signal.

How a survey works

      
A survey points a camera at many stars at once and measures each one's brightness again and again. Most stars never show a planet. Only planets whose orbits happen to line up with us can cross their star's face.

      
      

        
Why only a few transit

        
A planet transits only if its orbit is aligned closely enough with our line of sight. For random orientations the chance is about R★/a, the star's radius over the orbit's size (Winn 2010). Close-in planets around large stars transit more often.

Transit

      
When a planet crosses its star, it blocks a little light. The dip's depth gives the planet's size relative to the star; its repetition gives the period. This is what SetuHQ does: its run found pi Men c's transits in TESS sector 1. Open SetuHQ's Transit case ›

      
How a planet hides in the noise
Each transit of pi Men c dims its star by about 300 parts per million, for three hours, once every six days. In a month of measurements that is a handful of points among more than a thousand. Fold the month on the planet's period, so every transit lands on top of the others, and the dip stands out. This is SetuHQ's own stored light curve, not a simulation.

      
      

        
What shapes the dip

        
The depth is roughly (Rp/R★)². The star is brighter at its centre than its edge (limb darkening), so the bottom of the dip is rounded, not flat, and a central crossing dips deeper than k² (Mandel & Agol 2002; Claret 2017 coefficients for TESS). The four contact points set the total duration T14 and the flat-bottom duration T23 (Winn 2010).

Toward and away

      
Light from a star moving toward us arrives slightly bluer; moving away, slightly redder. Only the motion along our line of sight shifts the light. Motion across the sky (proper motion) shifts nothing.

      
      

        
How small the shift is

        
Δλ/λ = v_r/c, with v_r positive for motion away (redshift). For a wobble of tens of m/s the shift is a ten-millionth of the wavelength, so spectrographs measure it by cross-correlating thousands of lines (Fischer et al. 2016). The star's colour does not visibly change.

Radial velocity

      
A planet and its star both orbit their shared centre of mass, the barycentre. The star's small orbit makes its light shift back and forth: the radial-velocity curve. Its amplitude K gives the planet's minimum mass, m sin i.

      
      

        
The numbers

        
K = (2πG/P)^⅓ · Mp sin i / (M★ + Mp)^⅔ / √(1 − e²) (Gaudi & Winn 2007). The star's orbit is a★ = a · Mp/(M★ + Mp); the planet's is a · M★/(M★ + Mp). Eccentric orbits follow Kepler's equation, M = E − e sin E. Masses use the IAU nominal GM values (Prša et al. 2016).

Astrometry

      
The star's small orbit also moves its position on the sky. Astrometry measures that tiny loop, which gives the orbit's tilt and so the planet's true mass. The signal grows with the planet's mass and orbit and shrinks with distance.

Microlensing

      
When a foreground star (the lens) passes almost in front of a background star (the source), its gravity bends and magnifies the source's light for days to weeks. A planet around the lens adds a short extra spike. The lens itself is often too faint to see.

      
      

        
Point-lens magnification: A(u) = (u² + 2)/(u√(u² + 4)), with u the lens–source separation in Einstein radii; A(1) = 1.342. The Einstein angle is θ_E = √(κ M π_rel), κ ≈ 8.14 mas/M☉ (Gaudi 2012). A planetary spike lasts about √q · t_E, where q is the planet-to-star mass ratio. An event never repeats, so the planet cannot be re-observed this way.

Chapter 7 of 15
Blocking starlight

      
To photograph a planet, the starlight must be blocked. A Jupiter twin reflects about a billionth of its Sun's visible light; an Earth twin about 10⁻¹⁰ (Lawson et al. 2013). Young giant planets glow in the infrared and are far easier, which is why imaging finds young, wide, massive planets.

      
      

        
Blocking starlight

        
    
          
  1. History. Bernard Lyot invented the coronagraph in 1930 to study the Sun's corona without an eclipse; a focal-plane mask blocks the disc and a "Lyot stop" removes the diffracted light (Lyot 1939). SOHO's LASCO coronagraphs show the same idea every day (Brueckner et al. 1995). The Sun scene above is an illustration based on LASCO, not a LASCO image.
    2. 
          
  2. Coronagraph families. Lyot-type amplitude masks, apodised pupils and phase masks such as the vortex. The key figure is the inner working angle, in units of λ/D (Mawet et al. 2012). Earth at 10 pc sits 0.1″ from its star: about 5.3 λ/D for a 6 m telescope at 550 nm [calc].
    3. 
          
  3. Adaptive optics and speckles. A deformable mirror flattens the turbulent wavefront hundreds of times a second. What remains are speckles; angular differential imaging lets the sky rotate while speckles stay fixed, so they can be subtracted (Marois et al. 2006).
    4. 
          
  4. Starshade. A flower-shaped screen flown far in front of a telescope (Cash 2006). The Roman Starshade Rendezvous concept: 26 m at 26,000 km; HabEx: 52 m at about 76,600 km (HabEx Final Report).
    5. 
          
  5. Nulling interferometry. Two telescopes combined half a wave out of phase cancel the star on a dark fringe while an off-axis planet sits on a bright one (Bracewell 1978; the LIFE concept, Quanz et al. 2022).
    6. 
        

        
Coronagraph families, plane by plane

        
Each family below is drawn as four planes of one optical train. The intensities are computed, not painted: Fraunhofer optics of an ideal, monochromatic telescope with a clear circular pupil, shown on a log stretch from the star's peak down to a millionth. The mask and stop sizes, the apodiser and the planets' brightness are illustrative and say so.

        
      

      
      

        
    
          
  1. Lyot coronagraph. An opaque disc in the focal plane blocks the core of the star's image. What it does not remove is the light its edge diffracts, which the next lens sends to the rim of the re-imaged pupil; a Lyot stop slightly smaller than the pupil catches that rim (Lyot 1939). With a 4 λ/D disc, closing the stop from 150% to 80% of the pupil cuts the starlight at 8 λ/D from 6.7×10⁻⁵ to 1.8×10⁻⁷ of the star's peak (no coronagraph: 7.8×10⁻⁵) [calc], while a planet well outside the disc keeps 64% of its light, the stop's area.
    2. 
          
  2. Apodised pupil. Instead of a mask, the pupil's transmission tapers to its edge, so the image has no sharp edge to ring from. For A(ρ) = (1 − ρ²)² the brightest ring near 5 λ/D falls from 7.8×10⁻⁴ to 1.2×10⁻⁶ of the clear pupil's peak [calc], but only 1/(2n + 1) = 20% of the light gets through and the core is wider. Real apodisers (shaped pupils, prolate and APLC designs) are optimised numerically; this taper is illustrative.
    3. 
          
  3. Vortex phase mask. A transparent mask whose phase winds around its centre (e^(2iθ), charge 2) moves an on-axis star's light outside the re-imaged pupil: for an ideal charge-2 vortex on a clear circular aperture the field there is exactly zero inside the pupil and (1/ρ)² outside it [calc], so a stop just inside the pupil removes the star. Phase masks are the family that works closest to the star (Mawet et al. 2012); real vortices leave residuals from wavefront errors and the star's finite size.
    4. 
          
  4. Adaptive optics and ADI. A deformable mirror turns the seeing halo into a sharp core, but quasi-static speckles λ/D wide remain. With the instrument's pupil fixed, the sky and the planet turn through the night while the speckles stay; the median of the sequence models the speckles, and each frame minus it, turned back by its field rotation and stacked, keeps the planet (Marois et al. 2006). The frames on the stage are synthetic. On the sky, GPI reaches about 10⁻⁴ raw contrast at 0.4″ in 60 s and more than ten times deeper after an hour of ADI (Bailey et al. 2016).
    5.

Chapter 9 of 15
Radio

      
Magnetised planets shine at radio wavelengths: electrons spiralling down field lines into the auroral zones emit by the cyclotron maser, beamed in a hollow cone, up to the local cyclotron frequency (Zarka 1998; Treumann 2006). A close-in planet can also drive such emission on its star, as Io does on Jupiter (Zarka 2007). No exoplanet's own radio emission has been confirmed. Every claim below is tentative or contested, and says so.

      
      

        
The frequency scale

        
The cyclotron maser emits at, and just below, f_ce = eB/(2π m_e) ≈ 2.80 MHz per gauss of field at the source, so the highest frequency seen gives the field there. A weak planetary field emits below tens of MHz, where only low-frequency arrays such as LOFAR (10–240 MHz; van Haarlem et al. 2013) can listen; an M dwarf's kilogauss field reaches GHz.

Chapter 12 of 15
Molecules and life

      
Each gas absorbs at its own wavelengths. A molecule's shape sets how it vibrates, and its vibrations set where it absorbs in the infrared. No single gas proves life; a biosignature needs context.

      
      

        
A molecule with N atoms has 3N − 6 ways to vibrate (3N − 5 if it is straight): water has three, carbon dioxide four (its bend comes twice, at right angles), methane nine. A mode absorbs infrared light only if it changes the molecule's dipole. That is why CO₂'s symmetric stretch (7.50 µm), O₂'s stretch and two of methane's four fundamentals are silent in the infrared. The bands the modes make are the ones the Reading atmospheres chapter marks: water's two stretches at 2.66 and 2.73 µm make its 2.7 µm band, methane's ν₃ the 3.3 µm band, CO₂'s asymmetric stretch the 4.26 µm band, and CO's stretch the 4.67 µm band.

        
Band positions: the atlas's NIST CCCBDB fundamentals, λ = 10⁴/ν̃. Displacements are symmetry-correct sketches with the centre of mass fixed and no net spin, not a force-field calculation. In every mode the atom that moves most swings 0.2 Å, and the stage states how much that exaggerates its zero-point motion, the root-mean-square displacement from ħ/2ω at the mode's frequency: for water's hydrogens in ν₁ that is 0.047 Å, so they are drawn ×4. Time is slowed 10¹³ to 10¹⁴ times, and the stage gives the factor for each mode.

Population

      
With thousands of planets, patterns appear. Two of them: a valley in the sizes of small planets, and the zone around each star where liquid water could last.

      
      

        
Small close-in planets come in two groups: rocky super-Earths below about 1.5 R⊕ and gas-wrapped sub-Neptunes around 2–3 R⊕, with fewer in between (Fulton et al. 2017 found at least a factor-of-two deficit at 1.5–2.0 R⊕ in a carefully measured sample). The leading explanations strip thin envelopes from small cores: photoevaporation (Owen & Wu 2017) or core-powered mass loss (Gupta & Schlichting 2019).

        
These are raw catalogue counts (planets with a stored radius and period < 100 d; 40 log bins from 0.5 to 20 Earth radii), not occurrence rates: nothing corrects for which planets are easier to find, and catalogue radii carry the stars' radius errors. The dip is shallower here than in Fulton et al.'s corrected, precise sample. pi Men c's stored radius is 1.87 R⊕.

      

      
      

        
The habitable zone is the range of starlight in which an Earth-like planet could keep liquid surface water. Its limits come from climate models as a flux that depends on the star's temperature (Kopparapu et al. 2014). Being in it is necessary, not sufficient: it says nothing about a planet's atmosphere, and many planets inside it are giants.

The Seager equation

        
N = N★ · F_Q · F_HZ · F_O · F_L · F_S

        
It counts planets where a biosignature gas could be detected, not civilisations (Seager 2018): stars observed, the fraction quiet, with rocky planets in the habitable zone, observable, with life, and whose life makes a detectable gas.

        

        

        
Starting values: N★ = 30,000 M dwarfs bright enough for JWST, F_Q · F_HZ = 0.15, F_O = 0.001, F_L = 1, F_S = 0.5, giving N ≈ 2. They come from secondary summaries of Seager's talk and paper, not checked against the paper here; F_L = 1 is an assumption, not a measurement. The habitable-zone fraction links to the Population chapter's figures.

    2. The leaderboard decides which events lie on the TOI's ephemeris (the benchmark's score_star and match_toi) and counts control false alarms itself. Self-reported outcomes are not accepted.
    3. Calling every TOI a planet, with or without a flag, scores J = 0; a searchable star called not searched counts as not offered. J over every star of the scope is shown beside it.     5. A floor: the metadata-only baseline uses no light curve at all, only the public TOI number, period, duration, ephemeris errors and epoch, with a logistic regression fitted on the tuning half. A method that does not beat it is not shown to read the light curves better than the catalogue's own metadata predicts dispositions.

    Install and first run
    
        
    SetuHQ needs Python 3.11 or newer.
    
        
    pip install "setuhq[tess] @ git+https://github.com/ashfordeOU/SetuHQ"
setuhq run configs/showcase/tess-wasp-126-s1.toml --out runs/tess-wasp-126-s1   # or any star: copy a config, change its TIC and sectors
setuhq crossmatch runs/tess-wasp-126-s1          # the NASA Exoplanet Archive's planets for the star
setuhq site runs/tess-wasp-126-s1 --out site     # a website from your run (several runs: list them all)
    
        
    Every example on this site has its configuration in configs/ and its one-line command in docs/SHOWCASE.md. The run downloads the light curve from MAST, cleans it, lets the panel mark dips, vets each signal and writes every result with its provenance. The Probes run with no account; the Sentinels need an API key for their provider. Sentinels run on Claude (Anthropic) by default; other providers are supported and labelled. Probes are deterministic matched-filter detectors.
    
        
    Contribute a sweep
    
        
    A sweep searches every 2-minute target of a TESS sector and keeps every dip it finds, classified, with nothing thrown away. You can run a slice and send it in:
    
        
    setuhq sweep configs/sweep.toml --sector 5 --limit 200 --workers 3
setuhq bundle my-sweep/sector_0005.json.gz --contributor "Your Name" --out bundle/
    
        
    Open a pull request with the bundle. A maintainer checks it and merges it into Everything that dims, with your name on every entry it brings. See CONTRIBUTING.md.
    
        
    Use it from an AI assistant (MCP)
    
        
    pip install "setuhq[tess,mcp] @ git+https://github.com/ashfordeOU/SetuHQ"
setuhq-mcp --list-tools
    
        
    Set-up for each assistant is in docs/MCP-INSTALL.md.

    How the pictures are drawn.
    
        
    Every star on SetuHQ is drawn in its blackbody colour from its stored temperature. Every planet is an artist's impression: its look is chosen from two measured things, its radius (or, without one, its mass) and the light it gets from its star. Nothing in a picture is measured beyond those numbers, and composition never is.
    
      
    The rules
    
        
    Composition, from the radius in Earth radii (1 RJ = 11.209 R⊕):
    
        
    Drawn asRadiusWhy
    rockbelow 1.6 R⊕Rogers 2015, ApJ 801, 41: most planets larger than about 1.6 R⊕ are not rocky
    envelopebelow 4 R⊕the radius valley at 1.5–2.0 R⊕ (Fulton et al. 2017, AJ 154, 109); sub-Neptunes up to 4 R⊕
    neptunebelow 10 R⊕the catalogue's size-class boundary (Neptune-like 4–10 R⊕)
    giant10 R⊕ or morethe catalogue's size-class boundary (gas giant 10 R⊕ or more)
    
        
    Without a radius, from the mass (or m sin i, a minimum mass) in Earth masses; such a planet is drawn at a fixed size, marked not to scale. With neither, no planet is drawn.
    
        
    Drawn asMassWhy
    rockbelow 2 M⊕below 2 M⊕
    ambiguousbelow 10 M⊕2–10 M⊕: rock or envelope, not decidable from mass
    neptunebelow 50 M⊕10–50 M⊕
    giant50 M⊕ or more50 M⊕ or more
    
        
    Temperature band, from the equilibrium temperature Teq = 278.3 K × S1/4, where S = R★² (Teff / 5,772 K)⁴ / a² is the light the planet gets relative to Earth (zero albedo, full redistribution). Without a, a comes from Kepler's third law; without R★ or Teff, the catalogue's stored Teq is used; without that, the colours are neutral.
    
        
    BandTeqWhy
    coldbelow 200 Kwater ice is stable at the surface
    temperatebelow 350 Kspans the conservative habitable-zone fluxes; named temperate, not habitable
    warmbelow 1,000 Kbetween temperate and hot
    hotbelow 2,000 Kthe substellar point reaches √2·Teq ≥ 1,414 K, about the basalt solidus; hot Jupiters are conventionally ≥ 1,000 K
    ultrahot2,000 K or moreultra-hot Jupiters (Parmentier et al. 2018, A&A 617, A110)
    
        
    A star's colour: a Planck spectrum from 380 to 780 nm, through the CIE 1931 colour-matching functions (the multi-lobe fit of Wyman, Sloan and Shirley 2013), to sRGB, normalised so the colour is a hue, not a brightness. Limb darkening is the quadratic law with Claret's (2017) TESS-band coefficients, applied to a visible disc. Granulation and planet surfaces are texture only: decorative, never data.

    Star colour
    
        
    The colour is computed, not picked: Planck's law at the star's Teff, sampled from 380 to 780 nm in 5 nm steps, weighted by the CIE 1931 colour-matching functions (the multi-lobe fit of Wyman, Sloan and Shirley 2013), converted from XYZ to linear sRGB, normalised to the brightest channel and gamma-encoded. Teff is clamped to 1,000–40,000 K. Because of the normalisation every star is equally bright on screen: the colour is a hue, not a luminosity. The limb darkening is the quadratic law with Claret's (2017) coefficients for the TESS band, a red-optical law applied to a visible disc.
